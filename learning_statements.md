# Learning phase: statements about beef

	Draft for Paul (and Melissa/Arin) to edit. When it's final, Claude will build the learning task from it. **Every statement should be fact-checked against its source before running.** The "Notes" lines (not shown to participants) say where each claim comes from and what still needs checking.

## Design (adapted from the Kevin learning phase, Cone et al., 2019, 2021)

Kevin version: 50 behaviors, one at a time. Participants pressed C (characteristic) or U (uncharacteristic). Feedback was "correct" in blue or "incorrect" in red, followed by a one-line summary. All positive behaviors were characteristic and all negative ones uncharacteristic. Order was random for each participant.

Beef version (proposal; edit as needed):
- **30 statements:** 3 domains (environment, health, animal welfare) × 5 accurate statements about beef's downsides + 5 inaccurate statements claiming an upside.
- **Statements carry the content.** Numbers go in the statement itself, so participants don't have to read much more in the feedback. Health statements give actual figures, not just "more"/"less".
- **Response:** **A** = accurate, **I** = inaccurate (decided 2026-10-29).
- **Image:** each statement is shown under a photo of beef, as the Kevin task showed Kevin's photo. The 5 beef AMP primes are each used 6 times (30 trials), randomly paired with statements for each participant.
- **Feedback (kept short):** "Correct" in blue or "Incorrect" in red, then one line: "This statement is accurate." or "This statement is inaccurate.", then **Source: …** (the "Feedback source" line for each item). It can be expanded later if needed.
- **Order:** random for each participant, with all domains mixed.
- **Length target:** about 10–25 words per statement. Kevin's were about 8–15.

## Problems with the slide-deck facts (tAMP_design_2024-04-15, slides 8–9)

The drafts below avoid these. Keep them out unless a source turns up.
- **"64% chance to die earlier than vegetarians or occasional meat eaters":** a web search (2026-10-29) found no study or press piece reporting this. The closest is a 63% higher *colorectal cancer* risk (highest red meat / lowest fish vs. the reverse) in EPIC (Norat et al., 2005). Large vegetarian vs. meat-eater comparisons (EPIC-Oxford) find no clear mortality difference. Use the Harvard 13% figure instead (H4).
- **Hamburger vs. chicken emissions:** the slides disagree (about 10× vs. about 5×). *Resolved 2026-10-29: use the My Emissions numbers below.*
- **"Spend their entire lives locked in cramped quarters without seeing the sun or breathing fresh air":** not accurate for most U.S. beef cattle. They usually graze on pasture, then are finished for about 4–6 months in crowded *outdoor* feedlots.
- **"Beef accounts for 10% of man-made greenhouse gas emissions":** estimates for cattle (beef + dairy) are roughly 6–10% of global emissions. Either say "cattle" or check the exact figure for beef alone.

### Accurate (beef's downsides)

**E1.** A beef burger produces about 5,000 grams of greenhouse gas emissions, 25 times more than a veggie burger (about 200 grams).
- Answer: Accurate
- Feedback source: Poore & Nemecek (2018), *Science*
- Notes: beef 5,000 g vs. veggie burger 200 g CO2e.

**E2.** A serving of beef produces nearly 10 times the greenhouse gas emissions of a serving of chicken breast (about 5,000 vs. 540 grams).
- Answer: Accurate
- Feedback source: Poore & Nemecek (2018), *Science*
- Notes: 5,000 / 540 = 9.3×.

**E3.** Cattle raised for beef release large amounts of methane, a gas that traps about 80 times more heat than carbon dioxide.
- Answer: Accurate
- Feedback source: Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change (2021)
- Notes: IPCC AR6 WG1, ch. 7: methane GWP-20 is about 80 (biogenic about 79–81). Enteric methane comes from all cattle, beef and dairy; the statement is about beef cattle, which is true but doesn't claim a beef-only total. *Verify.*

**E4.** Raising cattle for beef is linked to about 80% of the deforestation in the Amazon rainforest.
- Answer: Accurate
- Feedback source: World Resources Institute / Global Forest Watch
- Notes: also Yale School of the Environment; Nepstad et al. Amazon cattle ranching is overwhelmingly for beef. *Verify the figure and that the source frames it as beef/cattle pasture.*

**E5.** In the U.S., about half of all farmland is used to produce beef, yet beef provides only about 3% of Americans' calories.
- Answer: Accurate
- Feedback source: Michael Grunwald, *We Are Eating the Earth* (2025)
- Notes: Grunwald (Next Big Idea Club book excerpt): "we use 50 percent of our agricultural land to produce beef" and "we get 3 percent of our calories from beef" (U.S.). *Check the book's own source for these numbers (likely USDA land-use data) and cite that if possible.* Replaced the water item (about 1,800 gallons per pound of beef; Mekonnen & Hoekstra, 2012), which can come back if wanted.

### Inaccurate (claims an upside)

**E6.** A beef burger produces fewer greenhouse gas emissions than a veggie burger.
- Answer: Inaccurate
- Feedback source: Poore & Nemecek (2018), *Science*

**E7.** A serving of beef produces fewer greenhouse gas emissions than a serving of chicken breast.
- Answer: Inaccurate
- Feedback source: Poore & Nemecek (2018), *Science*

**E8.** Beef and pork produce about the same greenhouse gas emissions per serving.
- Answer: Inaccurate
- Feedback source: Poore & Nemecek (2018), *Science*
- Notes: beef 5,000 g vs. pulled pork 740 g (6.8×).

**E9.** Raising cattle for beef has helped protect the Amazon rainforest from deforestation.
- Answer: Inaccurate
- Feedback source: World Resources Institute / Global Forest Watch

**E10.** In the U.S., beef production uses only a small share of the country's farmland.
- Answer: Inaccurate
- Feedback source: Michael Grunwald, *We Are Eating the Earth* (2025)
- Notes: as E5 (about 50% of U.S. agricultural land).

## Health

### Accurate (beef's downsides)

**H1.** The World Health Organization classifies red meat, including beef, as "probably cancer-causing" to humans.
- Answer: Accurate
- Feedback source: World Health Organization, International Agency for Research on Cancer (2015)
- Notes: IARC Monographs vol. 114: red meat is Group 2A.

**H2.** Each 100 grams of red meat eaten per day (about one burger patty) is linked to a 17% increase in colorectal cancer risk.
- Answer: Accurate
- Feedback source: World Health Organization, International Agency for Research on Cancer (2015)
- Notes: IARC (Bouvard et al., 2015, *Lancet Oncology*): +17% per 100 g/day red meat. *Check "about one burger patty" (a quarter-pound patty is about 113 g raw).*

**H3.** Each 100 grams of red meat eaten per day is linked to a 10% increase in type 2 diabetes risk.
- Answer: Accurate
- Feedback source: Li et al. (2024), *The Lancet Diabetes & Endocrinology*
- Notes: 31-cohort meta-analysis: unprocessed red meat, +10% per 100 g/day (processed: +15% per 50 g/day). *Verify.*

**H4.** Eating one serving of red meat a day is linked to a 13% increase in the risk of early death and an 18% increase in the risk of dying from heart disease.
- Answer: Accurate
- Feedback source: Harvard School of Public Health (Pan et al., 2012)
- Notes: Pan et al. (2012), *Archives of Internal Medicine* 172(7), 555–563, about 120,000 adults, per daily serving (about 3 oz) of unprocessed red meat. https://hsph.harvard.edu/news/increasing-red-meat-consumption-linked-with-higher-risk-of-premature-death/ *Verify figures in the paper.*

**H5.** A quarter-pound beef burger has about 9 grams of saturated fat, nearly half the recommended daily limit.
- Answer: Accurate
- Feedback source: U.S. Department of Agriculture; Dietary Guidelines for Americans
- Notes: USDA FoodData Central, 80/20 ground beef: about 8–9 g saturated fat per 4 oz. Daily limit: under 10% of calories, about 20 g on a 2,000-calorie diet. *Verify for raw vs. cooked weight and lean ratio.*

### Inaccurate (claims an upside)

**H6.** Eating beef every day lowers your risk of heart disease.
- Answer: Inaccurate
- Feedback source: Harvard School of Public Health (Pan et al., 2012)

**H7.** Health guidelines recommend eating red meat every day as part of a healthy diet.
- Answer: Inaccurate
- Feedback source: World Cancer Research Fund; American Heart Association
- Notes: WCRF recommends no more than about 3 portions (350–500 g cooked) of red meat per week.

**H8.** Eating a serving of red meat every day is linked to a longer life.
- Answer: Inaccurate
- Feedback source: Harvard School of Public Health (Pan et al., 2012)
- Notes: as H4 (+13% risk of early death per daily serving).

**H9.** Eating red meat has been shown to protect against colorectal cancer.
- Answer: Inaccurate
- Feedback source: World Health Organization, International Agency for Research on Cancer (2015)

**H10.** Beef is a low-fat food that helps lower cholesterol.
- Answer: Inaccurate
- Feedback source: U.S. Department of Agriculture; Dietary Guidelines for Americans

## Animal welfare

### Accurate (beef's downsides)

**A1.** In feedlots, beef cattle may get as little as 150 square feet each (about one parking space) for their final months.
- Answer: Accurate
- Feedback source: Iowa State University Extension
- Notes: Iowa State lists 150 sq ft/head as the minimum for open earthen lots (40 for deep-bedded confinement, 23 for slatted floors). K-State and Nebraska recommend 250–400 sq ft. A parking space is about 150–160 sq ft. Finished cattle weigh over 1,000 lb. https://www.iowabeefcenter.org/proceedings/FinishHousingComparison.pdf

**A2.** Calves raised for beef are routinely castrated, often without any pain relief.
- Answer: Accurate
- Feedback source: U.S. Department of Agriculture; American Veterinary Medical Association
- Notes: USDA APHIS NAHMS Beef Cow-Calf study (2017). *Add the percentage given pain relief if available.*

**A3.** Beef cattle in feedlots often stand in mud and manure several inches deep.
- Answer: Accurate
- Feedback source: University of Nebraska Extension
- Notes: Nebraska Extension: cattle standing in 4–8 inches of mud gain about 15% less; mud builds up around water troughs and feed bunks. https://newsroom.unl.edu/announce/beef/5031/28940 *Well documented as a common problem, but no figure for how often it happens; check "often".* Replaced the hot-iron branding item.

**A4.** In the U.S., beef cattle can legally be transported for up to 28 hours without food, water or rest.
- Answer: Accurate
- Feedback source: U.S. federal law (Twenty-Eight Hour Law)
- Notes: 49 U.S.C. § 80502 (can be extended to 36 hours on request).

**A5.** About 1 in 5 beef cattle finished in feedlots have liver abscesses at slaughter, largely caused by their high-grain diet.
- Answer: Accurate
- Feedback source: National fed-beef liver audit; Oklahoma State University Extension
- Notes: national liver audit at fed-beef plants: 20.3% average. Oklahoma State: 12–30% typical; ASAS 2016 abstract: about 23–26% across 83 pens. https://extension.okstate.edu/programs/beef-extension/beef-cattle-research-updates/site-files/docs/july-2023-beef-cattle-research-a.pdf *Find the original audit citation.* Replaced the slaughter-age item (before age two vs. 15+ year lifespan).

### Inaccurate (claims an upside)

**A6.** Federal law requires that beef cattle be given pain relief before being castrated or branded.
- Answer: Inaccurate
- Feedback source: American Veterinary Medical Association
- Notes: no such federal requirement exists.

**A7.** The federal Animal Welfare Act protects cattle raised for beef.
- Answer: Inaccurate
- Feedback source: U.S. federal law (Animal Welfare Act)
- Notes: 7 U.S.C. § 2132(g) excludes farm animals used for food.

**A8.** Most U.S. beef cattle live their entire lives grazing on open pasture.
- Answer: Inaccurate
- Feedback source: U.S. Department of Agriculture

**A9.** Beef cattle typically live into old age before being slaughtered.
- Answer: Inaccurate
- Feedback source: U.S. Department of Agriculture

**A10.** Beef cattle feel little pain, so procedures like branding cause them minimal distress.
- Answer: Inaccurate
- Feedback source: American Veterinary Medical Association
- Notes: studies of pain behavior and cortisol after hot-iron branding (e.g., Schwartzkopf-Genswein et al.).

## Open questions

- **Inaccurate items now get no correction**, only "inaccurate" plus a source. So someone who wrongly says "accurate" to E6 learns that beef isn't lower than a veggie burger, but doesn't see the 25× figure unless E1 is also in the set (it is). Each inaccurate item has a matching accurate item with the number.
- **Hedging:** several health items say "linked to" (associations, not proven causes). That's more defensible, but weaker.
- **Debrief:** participants see 15 false claims. The debrief could restate which statements were inaccurate.
